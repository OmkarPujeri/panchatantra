The Loss of Friends

In the city of Maidens' Delight, a merchant named Increase lived a life of virtue and wealth. He pondered one night and came to a conclusion: "Wealth, if not increased, will dwindle like soot. Even a small amount, if added to, will grow like an anthill. Therefore, wealth should be earned, guarded, and increased."

Increase collected merchandise for the city of Mathura and set out with his servants. He had two strong bulls, Joyful and Lively, to carry the load. As they traveled, Lively became exhausted and sank in the mud. The driver told Increase that Lively had sunk in the mud.

Increase was sad for a moment but continued his journey to Mathura. He left caretakers with Lively's food and said, "If he lives, bring him to me; if he dies, perform the last rites." The men, fearing the forest's dangers, made a false report that Lively had died.

In reality, Lively survived and hobbled to the bank of the Jumna River, where he regained his strength and became plump and energetic. He spent his days browsing on grass blades and tearing ant hills with his horns.

One day, a lion named Rusty came to the Jumna River with his retinue. He heard Lively's loud bellow and felt uneasy but hid his feelings. He gathered his company, including his two jackal friends, Cheek and Victor, under a banyan tree.

Rusty was a proud and powerful lion who relied on his strength and courage. He had no need for fancy trappings or education but ruled his kingdom with pride and heroism. His subjects looked up to him as their king.

Lively and Rusty soon became friends. They talked together every day. Lively was wise and taught the lion many things. Rusty took such delight in Lively that he began to neglect his own court.

Cheek and Victor, the two jackals, watched with worry. They had lost their place and their favor with the king. Victor said, "Let us separate them and win back the king." Cheek warned, "Meddling brings nothing but trouble." But Victor went to Rusty and told a lie. He said, "Lively plans to kill you and seize your throne." Then he went to Lively and told another lie. He said, "Rusty means to kill you." So the two friends grew suspicious and afraid of each other. They met in the forest and fought. Rusty killed Lively. When his anger had passed, Rusty looked at his dead friend and wept. He said, "Lively was my second life. In killing him, I have only hurt myself."

Moral: Greed and malice can lead to the loss of true friends.
