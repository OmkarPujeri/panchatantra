How the Crow-Hen Killed the Black Snake

The sun beat down on a great banyan tree, its massive branches stretching towards the sky. A crow and his wife made their home in a cozy nest, but their happiness was short-lived. A black snake lived in the tree's hollow trunk, and it devoured their chicks as soon as they were born.

The crow's wife, a wise and caring hen, couldn't bear the thought of losing their children. She fell at her husband's feet and begged him to leave the tree. "My dear lord, we can't stay here. It's not safe for our chicks. Let's find a new home where we can raise them safely."

The crow was torn. He loved their old tree, but his wife's words struck a chord. He knew she was right, but he couldn't shake off the feeling of loyalty to their familiar home. "We've lived here for a long time," he said. "I don't want to leave."

His wife's words finally convinced him to seek help. "I'll go and find someone who can help us," he said. "Maybe they can come up with a plan to get rid of the snake." He left his wife and set off to find a friend who could assist them.

The crow met a jackal who lived in a nearby tree. He told the jackal about the snake and their dilemma. The jackal listened thoughtfully and then said, "Don't worry, I have an idea. That snake is already on its way to its own downfall. Its cruel heart will be its own worst enemy."

The crow was curious. "How do you know that?" he asked. The jackal smiled and told a story about a heron who ate too many fish and was eventually strangled by a crab. "That snake is near its doom already," the jackal said. "But a shrewd device can be sure of it. Go to some place where a great king often passes. There, seize a golden chain or a necklace from a wealthy man who guards it carelessly. Drop it in the snake's hole, so that when it is recovered, the snake is killed."

The crow hurried home and told his wife. Together they flew off in search of a king's court. The crow-hen came upon a certain pond, where the women of a king were playing in the water. On the bank lay golden chains, pearl necklaces, garments, and gems. She seized one chain of gold and started back toward the tree.

But when the king's men saw the theft, they picked up clubs and ran after her. The crow-hen dropped the golden chain into the snake's hole and waited at a safe distance. When the king's men climbed the tree, they found a hole, and in it a black snake with a swelling hood. They killed him with their clubs, recovered the golden chain, and went their way. After that, the crow and his wife lived in peace.

Moral: When brute force will not serve, a shrewd device may still succeed.
