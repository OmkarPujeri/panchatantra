Crows and Owls

In the south stood a city called Earth-Base, and near it grew a great banyan tree. In its branches lived Cloudy, the king of the crows, with a countless flock. Not far away, in a mountain cave, lived Foe-Crusher, the king of the owls, with an army of his own.

The two kings were enemies. Whenever Foe-Crusher met a crow in the open, he killed it. Night after night he came to the banyan tree and left rings of dead crows beneath it. The crows could not see in the dark, and by day they could not find the owls' hidden cave.

Cloudy called his five counselors together and asked them what to do. He told them there were six choices: peace, war, retreat, entrenchment, alliance, or cunning.

Live-Again spoke first. He said the owl-king was too strong, and the crows should make peace.

Live-Well disagreed. The enemy was cruel and greedy, he said, and the crows should fight.

Live-Along said neither peace nor war would do. He advised a change of base, a retreat to gather strength.

Live-On said the crows should stay home and fence themselves in a fort.

Live-Long said they should find a powerful ally to stand against the owls.

Last of all, Cloudy turned to his oldest and wisest counselor, Live-Strong. The old crow had listened to every plan. He said the hour called for cunning, not any of the five.

"How, father?" asked Cloudy. "We do not even know where the owls live."

Live-Strong answered that through spies he would find their dwelling and their weak spot. A king, he said, sees through the eyes of his spies.

Then Live-Strong told Cloudy his plan. The crows must pretend to turn against him. Cloudy would insult him before them all, peck him, and smear him with blood. Then the crows would throw him out of the tree and fly away to Antelope Mountain. Live-Strong would go to the owls and win their trust. In secret he would find the heart of their fortress and destroy them. For the owls were blind in the daytime, and their cave had only one gate.

Cloudy did as he was told. He and his crows jeered at Live-Strong, pecked him, and covered him with blood. Then they cast him to the ground and flew off to Antelope Mountain.

Foe-Crusher's owls soon found the injured crow and carried him to their king. Live-Strong bowed low and said that Cloudy had treated him cruelly, and that he wished to serve the owls now. Foe-Crusher was pleased, and he took the clever crow into his cave.

The owl counselors were five in number, and their names were Red-Eye, Fierce-Eye, Flame-Eye, Hook-Nose, and Wall-Ear. Red-Eye alone grew suspicious. He warned the king that the crow was sly and would betray them.

But Foe-Crusher laughed at him. The other counselors said the crow could do no harm. They gave Live-Strong the best of the food, and he grew sleek and strong.

Live-Strong knew he could not act while the owls watched him. So he asked to live near the gate. Each day he carried one fagot of wood to the gate and piled it there, pretending to build himself a nest. The owls saw nothing wrong. Only Red-Eye understood what the pile was for, and at last, losing all hope, he flew away.

When the woodpile was high, Live-Strong slipped away at sunrise. The owls, blind in the day, did not see him go. He flew to Antelope Mountain and told Cloudy that the owls' cave was ready to burn. He told him to come, each crow carrying a burning brand.

Cloudy and his crows flew back at once, each with a fagot of fire in his beak. They dropped the flames on the pile at the gate. The fire leaped into the cave. The owls, blind in the sunlight, could not find their way out, and every one of them died.

So Cloudy won back his tree and his kingdom. The crows returned to the banyan tree, and never again did the owls trouble them.

Moral: Never trust an enemy, however friendly he may seem.
