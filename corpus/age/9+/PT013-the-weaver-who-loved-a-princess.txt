The Weaver Who Loved a Princess

In the Molasses Belt there was a city called Sugarcane City. In it lived two friends, a weaver and a carpenter. Both were masters of their crafts and earned more money than they needed. They wore soft, gay clothes and sweet scents. Every day they worked nine hours, then met to enjoy the city together. So their pleasant time passed.

One day a great festival filled the streets with people in their finest ornaments. The two friends strolled through the squares and temples. There the weaver caught sight of a princess at the window of a stucco palace. She was lovely beyond words, and the love-god pierced him with five fierce arrows. He hid his feelings and tottered home, seeing nothing but her. All night he lay on his unmade bed, sighing and reciting poetry, unable to sleep.

Next day the carpenter came as usual. He found his friend sprawled on the bed, pale and tearful. He asked what was wrong, but the weaver was too ashamed to speak. At last the carpenter, skilled at reading symptoms, took his hand and said, "Comrade, if I am right, this is not a fever but love." Then the weaver sat up and poured out the whole story.

The carpenter reflected, then asked, "The king is a warrior, and you are a merchant. Have you no reverence for the holy law?" The weaver replied that the law allowed such longings in an honest heart. Seeing his friend was determined, the carpenter asked, "What will you do next?" The weaver answered, "I do not know. I told you because you are my friend." To that he would add nothing more.

At last the carpenter said, "Rise, bathe, eat, and put away your despair. I will invent something so that you may enjoy her love at once." Hope revived, the weaver returned to his usual way of living.

The next day the carpenter brought a brand-new mechanical bird, shaped like Garuda, the bird of Vishnu. It was carved of wood, painted, and fitted with clever plugs. "Mount it and insert a plug," he said, "and it flies wherever you wish. Pull the plug out, and it alights. Tonight, when all are asleep, dress yourself as Vishnu, ride this bird, and land on the maidens' balcony of the palace. The princess sleeps there alone. Arrange with her whatever you please."

So when night fell clear, the weaver bathed, used incense and powders, and adorned himself with a diadem and rich jewelry. Then he followed his friend's instructions.

The princess lay alone on the balcony, bathed in moonlight, her mind idly turning to love. Suddenly she saw the weaver, disguised as Vishnu and mounted on his heavenly bird. She sprang from her bed, adored his feet, and said humbly, "O Lord, why am I honored by this visit? Command me."

He answered in sweet, stately tones, "You yourself, dear maiden, are the reason for my visit to earth." She said, "But I am only a mortal girl." He replied, "No, you were my bride and fell to earth by a curse. I have long protected you from other men. Now I will marry you by the ceremony used in heaven." She agreed, for it was beyond her fondest hopes, and he married her.

So day followed day in delight. Before dawn the weaver would mount his bird, say, "I return to Vishnu's heaven," and reach home unseen.

Yet one day the guards of the women's quarters saw signs that the princess was meeting a man. Trembling for their lives, they reported the matter to the king.

The king, troubled, sent the queen to look into it. When the queen found her daughter's lips sore from kissing, she cried, "You wicked girl! You are a disgrace to the family! Who is this man? Tell me the truth." Ashamed, the princess told the whole story of the weaver disguised as Vishnu.

But the queen was delighted, not angry. Laughing, she rushed to the king and said, "O King, you are fortunate! It is blessed Vishnu himself who comes each night to our daughter. He has married her by the ceremony used in heaven. Tonight we must hide in the window niche and see him. But he does not speak with mortals." Believing this, the king waited through a day that felt a hundred years long.

That night, hidden in the niche, the king watched the sky. Presently he saw a figure descend from heaven on Garuda, holding the conch, discus, and mace, marked with the familiar signs. Drenched with joy, he told the queen, "None on earth is so blessed as we! Through our son-in-law's power, I shall conquer the whole world."

Now envoys arrived to collect the yearly tribute for King Valor, ruler of the south. Proud of his new relationship with Vishnu, the king showed them no honor. The envoys grew angry. "Pay-day is past," they said. "Why have you failed to pay your taxes? You must have gained some new and supernatural power, to treat King Valor, who is a flame and a serpent, so lightly." The king answered them by showing them his bare bottom. They returned home, exaggerated the matter beyond all measure, and stirred their master's wrath.

King Valor marched against the city with a great army and ravaged the countryside. The survivors gathered at the palace gate and taunted the king, but he felt no fear.

When the enemy invested Sugarcane City, the king's counselors begged him to take the danger seriously. "Be at ease," said the king. "I have found a way to kill this foe. Tomorrow morning you will see." Then he summoned his daughter. "Dear child," he coaxed, "tonight, when blessed Vishnu comes, ask him to kill the enemy in the morning."

Lovely carried this message to the weaver. He laughed. "Dear love, what a small matter, a war with men! Long ago I slew great demons by the thousand, armed with magic. There was Hiranyakashipu, and Kansa, and Madhu, and Kaitabha, to name a few. Go and tell the king: Dismiss your fear. In the morning Vishnu will slay the enemy host with his discus."

She did, and the king was overjoyed. He had it proclaimed through the whole city that whatever anyone seized from the enemy's camp tomorrow would be his own. The citizens rejoiced.

But the weaver fell to brooding. "What am I to do? If I fly away, I shall never see my wife again, and Valor will drag her off after killing her father. If I fight, I shall meet death. Either way, death. It is better, then, to die game. Perhaps, seeing me ride Garuda, the enemy will think me the true Vishnu and flee."

Meanwhile the true Garuda carried a report to the true Vishnu in heaven. "O Lord," he said, "a weaver on earth has disguised himself as you and married a princess. A stronger king from the south now marches against her father. If the weaver dies in battle, the world will believe that blessed Vishnu was killed by a mortal, and the faith of the people will fail. The decision rests with you." Vishnu meditated, then said, "Your reasoning is just. This weaver has a spark of divinity, and he must slay yonder king. To do it, we must befriend him. My spirit shall enter his body, you shall inspire his bird, and my discus shall become his discus." "So be it," said Garuda.

So the weaver, inspired by Vishnu, prepared for battle. He performed the auspicious ceremonies and put on ornaments fit for war. At sunrise, to the roll of war-drums, he rose from the palace roof on Garuda, scattering gold and jewels, while the townspeople gazed up in wonder. He hovered above the army and blew a grand burst from Vishnu's conch.

At that blare, elephants, horses, chariots, and foot-soldiers were stunned. Many fled with shrill screams. Some rolled on the ground, unable to move. Some stood frozen, their terrified eyes fixed on the sky.

Even the gods gathered to watch, and Brahma himself was astonished. He could not see through the trick that the weaver had devised.

Then the weaver hurled his discus at Valor. It cut the king in two, then flew back to his hand. At the sight, all the other kings leaped from their chariots and bowed low, pleading, "O Lord, our leaderless army is destroyed. Spare our lives and command us."

He answered, "Your lives are safe. Whatever King Stout-Mail commands, you must do at once." The kings humbly agreed. The weaver gave Stout-Mail all the wealth of the defeated king, and he himself lived in all delight with his princess.

Moral: The gods befriend the man who climbs the height of determination.
