Soft, the Weaver

In a small town, Soft, a skilled weaver, made beautiful garments for princes. Despite his hard work, he struggled to earn more than just enough food and clothes for himself and his wife. He saw other weavers making coarse fabrics and earning a lot of money, which made him think of moving to a new city.

His wife, wise and understanding, tried to persuade him to stay. She believed that fate decided how much wealth a person would have, and that traveling wouldn't change their fortunes. She shared her thoughts with him, but Soft was determined to leave.

Soft moved to Growing City, where he worked hard and eventually saved three hundred gold-pieces. On his way back home, he stopped in a forest to rest. As he slept, he had a strange dream. In the dream, two figures, Doer and Deed, argued about Soft's wealth. Doer, who seemed to be in charge, said that Soft deserved the money he earned. Deed, on the other hand, claimed that he was responsible for giving Soft the money.

Soft woke up, feeling puzzled and worried. He searched his bag and found it empty. He thought, "Oh no! All my hard work for nothing!" He decided to return to Growing City, where he earned even more money, this time five hundred gold-pieces, in just one year.

On his way back, he stopped at the same banyan tree and had another strange dream. The two figures reappeared, arguing about Soft's new wealth. This time, Doer defended Soft's right to the money. Soft woke up again, feeling frustrated and hopeless. He thought, "What's the point of earning money if I'll just lose it?" He decided to hang himself from the banyan tree.

Just as he was about to let himself drop, a figure appeared in the sky. It was the one responsible for taking Soft's money. The figure told Soft to go home and not be so quick to give up. Soft asked for plenty of money, but the figure explained that he wouldn't be able to enjoy it or give it away. Soft replied, "Even if I can't use it, I want it for the sake of having it."

"If that is how matters stand," said the figure, "go once more to Growing City. Two merchants' sons live there, Penny-Hide and Penny-Fling. Watch how each of them lives, then ask for the nature of one or the other." With that the figure vanished, and Soft returned to Growing City.

By evening twilight he found Penny-Hide's house and went in. Though the wife and children scolded him, he made his way to the courtyard and sat down. At dinner he was given food but no kind word, and he slept there. That night he saw the same two figures holding council, and when he woke he had to fast, for Penny-Hide had fallen ill.

So Soft left and went to Penny-Fling's house, where he was welcomed warmly and given food, clothes, and a comfortable bed. That night the two figures argued again, and at dawn a policeman came with money from the king and gave it all to Penny-Fling.

Then Soft understood. Penny-Fling, with no capital at all, was a better man than stingy old Penny-Hide. So he asked the Lord of All to make him a person whose money went in gifts and fun.

Moral: The true value of wealth lies not in its accumulation, but in the ability to enjoy and share it with others.
