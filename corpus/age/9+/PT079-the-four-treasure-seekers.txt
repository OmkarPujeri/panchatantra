The Four Treasure-Seekers

In a small town, four Brahmans lived as the best of friends. They were poor and struggled to make ends meet. One day, they had a heart-to-heart conversation about their situation. They decided that it was better to be dead than to live in poverty.

The Brahmans discussed various ways to make money, but they agreed that the only reliable method was through trade. They learned that trade had seven branches, including false weights and balances, price-boosting, and foreign trade. However, they also discovered that these methods were not always honest and could lead to trouble.

The Brahmans decided to try their luck in foreign trade. They traveled to the Avanti country, where they met a master-magician named Terror-Joy. He told them that he could give them magic quills that would lead them to treasure. The Brahmans were skeptical, but they decided to give it a try.

The magician gave each of them a magic quill and told them to go to the northern slope of the Himalaya Mountains. The quill would drop, and wherever it did, they would find treasure. The Brahmans followed the magician's instructions and soon found themselves in a beautiful but treacherous landscape.

As they journeyed on, the first quill dropped, revealing a treasure of copper. The Brahmans were overjoyed, but they soon realized that copper was not enough to solve their poverty problem. They decided to continue their search, and the second quill dropped, revealing a treasure of silver. Again, they were disappointed, thinking that gold must be ahead.

The third quill dropped, revealing a treasure of gold. The Brahmans were ecstatic, but they soon realized that gold was not the answer to their problems either. They decided to continue their search, and the fourth quill dropped, revealing a terrible sight: a man with a wheel whirling on his head.

The Brahmans were shocked and amazed. They asked the wheel-bearer how he had ended up in such a state, and he told them the story of how he had come to the place, just like them, and had been tortured by the wheel. The Brahmans realized that they had been foolish to think that treasure was the answer to their problems.

The gold-finder scolded his companion, saying, "I told you not to go any further. But you did not listen. Indeed, there is wisdom in the saying: 'Scholarship is less than sense; therefore seek intelligence.' You were blinded by your desire for treasure and did not use your common sense."

The wheel-bearer would not agree. Fate rules us all, he said. Even a very wise man may be struck down. A very foolish man may live happily, if fate protects him.

The gold-finder answered that this was partly true. Good fortune does come through fate. Yet a man should still make his own fate, and never give up good sense. Then the gold-finder said farewell to his friend and started home.

Moral: True wisdom and common sense are more valuable than wealth and material possessions.
