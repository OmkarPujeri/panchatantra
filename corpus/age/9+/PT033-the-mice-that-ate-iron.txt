The Mice That Ate Iron

In a certain town, a merchant named Naduk lived. He had lost his money and decided to travel abroad. A man who has lost his money cannot live in places where he once spent money like a rich man.

Naduk had an iron balance-beam in his house. It weighed a thousand pails and was passed down from his ancestors. He put it in pawn with Merchant Lakshman before he left for foreign lands.

After traveling for a long time, Naduk returned to his native city and asked Lakshman to return his balance-beam. But Lakshman said, "I'm sorry, Naduk, but the balance-beam has been eaten by mice."

Naduk said, "Lakshman, it's not your fault. Such is life. Nothing stays the same. I'm going to the river for a bath. Please send your son Money-God with me to carry my bathing things."

Lakshman was guilty and said, "My son, let's go with Uncle Naduk to the river." Money-God was happy to go with Naduk. After Naduk bathed, he pushed Money-God into a mountain cave and blocked the entrance with a big rock. Then he returned to Lakshman's house.

When Lakshman asked about his son, Naduk said, "A hawk carried him off from the river-bank." Lakshman was furious and said, "Naduk, you're lying! A hawk can't carry off a big boy like Money-God!"

Naduk replied, "But the mice ate a balance-beam made of iron. If you want your son back, give me the balance-beam." They took their dispute to the palace gate, and Lakshman cried out for help.

The magistrates said to Naduk, "Return Lakshman's son." Naduk said, "I'm telling the truth! A hawk carried him off." But the magistrates said, "You're not telling the truth. How can a hawk carry off a 15-year-old boy?"

Naduk laughed and said, "Gentlemen, listen to me. If mice can eat a balance-beam of iron, a hawk can steal an elephant. A boy is light as a feather."

The magistrates laughed and asked Naduk to explain. He told them the story of the balance-beam, and they laughed too. They ordered the balance-beam and Money-God to be returned to their rightful owners.

Moral: A liar is often trapped by his own lie.
